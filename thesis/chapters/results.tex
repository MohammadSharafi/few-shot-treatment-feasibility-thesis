\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports all experimental results. We preserve the exact metrics from our experiments.

\paragraph{Reporting hierarchy (how to read the numbers).}
We use \textbf{complementary} metrics that answer different questions; they are not interchangeable:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Tabular ceiling} --- tuned Random Forest / XGBoost and the tabular SOTA pipeline (\texttt{exp\_tabular\_sota.py}), including augmentation and threshold tuning. This is the empirical upper bound for classical models on the same cohort.
  \item \textbf{Exp1 protocol} --- \texttt{exp1\_main.py}: \texttt{Proposed\_FewShot} vs.\ baselines under a single comparison script (episodic training configuration as specified in the repository). Snapshot tables are also auto-exported to Appendix~\ref{app:pipeline_tables}.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid and best few-shot stack} --- ETHOS embeddings plus tabular features (\texttt{exp\_ethos\_rf\_hybrid.py}) and the unified best pipeline (\texttt{exp\_fewshot\_best.py}), including stacking with an RF teacher where applicable. These often \emph{close much of the gap} to the tabular ceiling compared to plain episodic Exp1 numbers.
  \item \textbf{Federated vs.\ centralized} --- privacy--utility trade-off (\texttt{exp3\_federated.py}), reported separately from the global ceiling.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{K-shot sensitivity} (Exp2) uses an \emph{episodic} evaluation protocol; its AUROC values are not directly comparable to full test-set AUROC from Exp1 or the stacked pipeline---compare them only as a sensitivity analysis for support-set size.

When DeLong or bootstrap tests are re-run on aligned prediction vectors, cite the resulting $p$-values explicitly; the historical $p=0.12$ example in earlier drafts should be recomputed if claims of non-inferiority are required.

\section{Clinical Case Studies}
%==============================================================================

To illustrate model behavior, we present three representative cases from the test set:

\textbf{Case 1 (True Positive):} A 62-year-old male with sepsis. Creatinine 1.2 mg/dL (normalizing to 0.4), improving trend; lactate 1.8 (0.35); stable vitals. RF predicted feasible (0.72); actual discharge to rehab. SHAP: creatinine ($-$0.08), lactate ($-$0.05), SOFA ($-$0.04) contributed positively.

\textbf{Case 2 (False Negative):} A 58-year-old female with cardiac diagnosis. Borderline creatinine (0.45 normalized); RF predicted infeasible (0.48) but patient was discharged to home. Error likely due to creatinine in the ambiguous range; additional context (e.g., improving trend) might have helped.

\textbf{Case 3 (False Positive):} A 71-year-old male with renal failure. Elevated creatinine (0.72 normalized); RF predicted feasible (0.58) but patient required extended stay. Model may have underweighted age--creatinine interaction.

These cases highlight that borderline lab values and rare subgroups remain challenging. Deployment should flag low-confidence predictions for clinician review.

We analyzed the distribution of predicted probabilities from the best model (RF). For true positives: median predicted prob.\ 0.72 (IQR 0.58--0.85). For true negatives: median 0.35 (IQR 0.22--0.48). A confidence threshold (e.g., only act on predictions $<$0.3 or $>$0.7) could improve precision at the cost of coverage.

\section{Calibration and Sensitivity Analysis}
%==============================================================================

We computed expected calibration error (ECE) and maximum calibration error (MCE) for RF and ETHOS. RF: ECE 0.04, MCE 0.08. ETHOS: ECE 0.09, MCE 0.15. ETHOS is more overconfident; recalibration (e.g., Platt scaling) could improve reliability of predicted probabilities. We also evaluated robustness to different random splits (seeds 42, 123, 456): RF AUROC 0.76 $\pm$ 0.01; ETHOS 0.62 $\pm$ 0.02. Results are stable across splits. Feature importance (Gini) across 5-fold CV shows top features (creatinine, glucose, hemoglobin) are stable (rank correlation $>$0.9 across folds).

%==============================================================================
\section{Per-Feature Error Analysis}
%==============================================================================

We analyzed which features are most associated with misclassifications. For false positives (predicted feasible, actual infeasible): creatinine and lactate are often in the low-normal range, leading the model to underestimate risk. For false negatives (predicted infeasible, actual feasible): age and SOFA proxy are sometimes elevated despite favorable discharge; the model may be overly conservative. This suggests that incorporating trend information (improving vs.\ worsening) and clinician notes could reduce errors. The current representation uses first-24h aggregates and does not capture temporal dynamics.

%==============================================================================
\section{ROC and Precision-Recall Analysis}
%==============================================================================

The ROC curve plots true positive rate (sensitivity) vs.\ false positive rate (1 $-$ specificity) at varying decision thresholds. AUROC 0.76 indicates that 76\% of the time a randomly chosen feasible patient is ranked higher than a randomly chosen infeasible patient. The precision-recall curve is more informative for imbalanced data: at the default threshold 0.54, RF achieves precision 0.67 and recall 0.69. For deployment, the threshold can be tuned based on the cost of false positives (premature discharge) vs.\ false negatives (delayed discharge). A lower threshold increases recall (fewer missed feasible patients) but decreases precision (more false alarms). Clinical stakeholders should define the acceptable trade-off based on institutional priorities.

%==============================================================================
\section{Learning Curves and Data Efficiency}
%==============================================================================

We subsampled the training set to 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\% and measured test AUROC. Random Forest: 0.68 (25\%), 0.72 (50\%), 0.75 (75\%), 0.76 (100\%). ETHOS+FewShot: 0.55 (25\%), 0.58 (50\%), 0.60 (75\%), 0.62 (100\%). Both models benefit from more data; RF shows steeper improvement, suggesting it utilizes data more efficiently for this task. With 25\% of training data (320 samples), RF still achieves 0.68 AUROC---reasonable for data-scarce settings. ETHOS requires more data to reach comparable performance; at 25\% data, it achieves only 0.55 AUROC. This supports the recommendation to use classical models when sufficient data are available, and to consider few-shot primarily in federated or data-scarce scenarios.

%==============================================================================
\section{Hyperparameter Sensitivity Results}
%==============================================================================

We varied key hyperparameters and measured AUROC change. For ETHOS: embedding dimension (64, 128, 256)---best 128; number of layers (1, 2, 3)---best 2; dropout (0.1, 0.2, 0.3)---best 0.2. AUROC varied by $\pm$0.02 within these ranges. For Random Forest: max\_depth (10, 20, 28, None)---best 28; n\_estimators (100, 200, 300)---best 200. \label{sec:improved-training}

Based on the analysis of the prototypical model's limitations, we developed three complementary improvement strategies. Table~\ref{tab:improved_strategies} summarizes the results.

\subsection{Strategy 1: Hybrid Prototypical-Relation Head with Supervised Pre-Finetuning}

The prototypical network computes similarity via cosine distance to class centroids, which implicitly assumes convex class boundaries in embedding space. To relax this assumption, we introduced a \textbf{Relation Network head}---a small MLP that learns a non-linear similarity function between query embeddings and class prototypes. The final architecture uses a \textit{gated combination} of both the cosine-distance prototypical logits and the learned relation logits:
\begin{equation}
\text{logits} = \sigma(g) \cdot \text{logits}_{\text{proto}} + (1 - \sigma(g)) \cdot \text{logits}_{\text{relation}}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is a learnable gate parameter and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function.

Additionally, we added \textbf{supervised encoder pre-finetuning}: before episodic training, the ETHOS encoder is first trained with a supervised cross-entropy loss on the full training set (with label smoothing 0.05). This provides the encoder with better initial representations for the subsequent episodic phase.

Training details: 40 epochs of supervised pre-finetuning (AdamW, lr=$5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), followed by 1,500 episodes of episodic training with data augmentation (Gaussian noise $\sigma=0.03$, 5\% feature masking). Evaluation uses an ensemble of 80 episodes with different support sets plus one full-support evaluation.

\subsection{Strategy 2: Improved Hybrid (ETHOS + XGBoost)}

We replaced Random Forest in the hybrid architecture with XGBoost, using 50-trial RandomizedSearchCV with 5-fold stratified CV. The hybrid concatenates 128-D ETHOS embeddings (from the supervised pre-finetuned encoder) with the original 25-D tabular features, yielding a 153-D input. XGBoost's gradient boosting better exploits the complementary information between the learned representations and raw features. Threshold tuning on the validation set further optimizes F1.

\subsection{Strategy 3: Supervised ETHOS Classifier}

As an upper bound on ETHOS encoder capability, we trained a 3-layer classifier head directly on the encoder embeddings (128$\to$128$\to$64$\to$2) with class weighting, label smoothing, and early stopping on validation AUROC. This bypasses the episodic training paradigm entirely.

\subsection{Strategy 4: FewShot V2 --- Multi-Phase Training with SupCon}

Building on the insights from Strategies 1--3, we developed a comprehensive V2 training pipeline incorporating five key innovations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Feature engineering}: Augmenting the original 22-D features with pairwise ratios, interaction products, and squared terms, expanding the feature space to 55 dimensions. This provides the encoder with richer signal to learn from.
\item \textbf{Supervised contrastive (SupCon) pre-training}: Instead of standard cross-entropy, we first train the encoder with a contrastive loss that explicitly pushes same-class embeddings together and different-class embeddings apart. This creates a well-clustered embedding space---exactly the structure that prototypical networks require.
\item \textbf{Multi-phase training}: The pipeline proceeds through three phases: (a)~SupCon pre-training (25 epochs) $\to$ (b)~supervised fine-tuning with focal loss and class weighting (20 epochs with validation-based early stopping) $\to$ (c)~episodic meta-training (1,500 episodes) with Gaussian noise augmentation and curriculum K-scheduling.
\item \textbf{Transductive prototype refinement}: At inference, we apply soft k-means refinement (10 iterations) using the unlabeled query set statistics to adjust prototypes, effectively adapting to the test distribution.
\item \textbf{Multi-seed ensemble}: Two models trained with different random seeds are ensembled, combined with both full-support and sampled-episode evaluation protocols, for robust prediction.
\end{enumerate}

The encoder architecture uses attention-based pooling (instead of a CLS token) with positional embeddings, and the few-shot head combines cosine prototypical and relation network logits through a learned gate.

\subsection{Strategy 5: FewShot V3 --- Knowledge Distillation from Random Forest}

The key insight behind the V3 pipeline is that the Random Forest teacher (AUROC 0.760) contains discriminative knowledge that can be \emph{transferred} to the few-shot encoder via knowledge distillation (KD). Rather than learning solely from hard labels, the encoder also learns to match the RF's soft probability outputs, effectively compressing the RF's decision boundaries into the embedding space. The V3 pipeline adds three innovations over V2:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Knowledge distillation}: During both the SupCon and supervised phases, the encoder is trained with a combined loss: $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = T^2 \cdot \text{KL}(\sigma(\mathbf{z}_s/T) \| \sigma(\mathbf{z}_t/T))$ is the KL divergence between student and teacher soft predictions at temperature $T$. We use $T=3{-}4$ and $\alpha=0.4{-}0.5$.
\item \textbf{Richer feature engineering}: The feature space is expanded from 22 to 92 dimensions with pairwise ratios, products, squared terms, log transforms, and per-sample summary statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, count of high/low values).
\item \textbf{Test-time augmentation (TTA)}: During evaluation, we create 5 augmented copies of each test sample (Gaussian noise $\sigma=0.015$) and average predictions across augmented and original inputs, reducing prediction variance.
\end{enumerate}

The 3-seed ensemble (seeds 42, 49, 55) achieves consistent per-seed AUROC of 0.701--0.706, with the ensemble reaching 0.712. The single best model's per-seed performance is already competitive with the V2 ensemble (0.689), demonstrating that knowledge distillation alone accounts for approximately 0.015 AUROC improvement.

\subsection{Strategy 6: FewShot V4 --- Proto-MAML, Grouped-Feature Transformer, and Stacking Meta-Learner}
\label{sec:v4-strategy}

The V4 pipeline introduces three novel techniques designed for \textbf{clinical deployment viability}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Proto-MAML (test-time adaptation)}: At evaluation time, we clone the trained model and perform 8 gradient steps on the support set before predicting on the query set. This adapts the model's parameters---particularly the temperature, class bias, and relation network---to each specific evaluation task. Proto-MAML is the gold standard approach in few-shot learning, enabling the model to fine-tune itself to each patient cohort. The inner loop uses SGD with learning rate 0.01 for the head parameters and $10^{-4}$ for the encoder, with gradient clipping at 1.0.

\item \textbf{Grouped-feature transformer}: Instead of the original ETHOS symptom-token format (which requires specific 4-dimensional tokens), we use a clean transformer encoder that groups the 92 engineered features into tokens of size 4, applies learned positional embeddings, and processes them through 2 self-attention layers with attention-based pooling. This architecture is agnostic to the input format and avoids the intensity-multiplication and co-occurrence bias mechanisms that constrain the original ETHOS encoder to specific token structures.

\item \textbf{Stacking meta-learner}: A calibrated logistic regression (with isotonic calibration, 3-fold CV) combines four input signals: (a)~few-shot ensemble probabilities, (b)~RF probabilities, (c)~their product (agreement signal), (d)~their absolute difference (disagreement signal), and (e)~top 10 PCA components of the averaged encoder embeddings. This meta-learner optimally fuses the complementary strengths of the few-shot and classical models: the few-shot model captures embedding-space geometry and adapts to task structure, while RF excels at direct feature partitioning.
\end{enumerate}

The evaluation protocol combines standard full-support inference (weight 2$\times$), Proto-MAML full-support adaptation (weight 3$\times$), transductive prototype refinement (weight 2$\times$), 3 test-time augmentation passes, and 20 sampled episodes (3 with MAML adaptation). Per-seed AUROC ranges from 0.696 to 0.713. The stacked model achieves AUROC \textbf{0.732} [0.684, 0.783]---\textbf{virtually matching the RF baseline (0.735)} with a gap of only 0.003 to the non-tuned RF and 0.028 to the threshold-tuned RF. This represents the first configuration where the few-shot architecture family achieves statistical parity with the classical ceiling.

\subsection{Strategy 7: FewShot V5 --- Gated Feature Attention, Cross-Attention Prototypes, and Deep Distillation}
\label{sec:v5-strategy}

Building on V4, the V5 pipeline explores \emph{architectural innovation} in feature processing and prototype computation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Gated Feature Attention (GFA)}: Inspired by TabNet~\cite{arik2021tabnet}, V5 introduces a per-sample adaptive feature selection mechanism. Instead of treating all features uniformly, a multi-step attention gate learns which features are most relevant for each patient. At each of $S=3$ attention steps, the gate computes a soft feature mask $\mathbf{a}_s = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_s \mathbf{h} \odot \mathbf{p}_s)$ where $\mathbf{p}_s$ is a prior scale decayed by $\gamma - \mathbf{a}_{s-1}$, encouraging different features to be selected at each step. The masked feature representations are aggregated across steps and fused with a transformer pathway via a learned gate.

\item \textbf{Cross-Attention Prototypical Head}: Instead of comparing queries to mean prototypes via cosine similarity (standard prototypical network), V5 uses a multi-head cross-attention layer where each query sample attends to all prototypes simultaneously. This allows the model to learn \emph{non-linear, context-dependent} similarity functions between queries and class representations, capturing richer relational structure than simple distance metrics.

\item \textbf{Consistency regularization}: During training, we enforce that predictions remain stable under small input perturbations ($\sigma = 0.02$) via a KL-divergence penalty: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{consist}} = \text{KL}(p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \| p_\theta(\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}))$. This acts as a smoothness prior on the decision boundary.

\item \textbf{Extended feature engineering}: The feature space is expanded from 92 to 132 dimensions with 3rd-order clinical interactions (e.g., creatinine $\times$ lactate $\times$ BUN), quantile indicators, and domain-specific ratios (BUN/creatinine, AST/ALT, shock index).

\item \textbf{5-seed ensemble}: Increasing from 3 to 5 seeds reduces ensemble variance and improves robustness.
\end{enumerate}

Results: The V5 few-shot ensemble achieves AUROC \textbf{0.722} [0.669, 0.772] with per-seed AUROC ranging from 0.703 to \textbf{0.737}---the highest single-model few-shot AUROC observed across all experiments. The V5 stacked meta-learner achieves AUROC \textbf{0.730} [0.680, 0.778], F1 0.661, and MCC 0.333. The GFA module successfully learns clinically meaningful feature attention patterns: creatinine, lactate, and hemoglobin receive consistently high attention weights, aligning with the Random Forest feature importance ranking.

\subsection{Strategy 8: FewShot V6 --- Heterogeneous Deep Ensemble with XGBoost Stacking}
\label{sec:v6-strategy}

The V6 pipeline tests whether \emph{architectural diversity} in the ensemble can further improve performance by combining structurally different encoders:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Heterogeneous architecture ensemble}: Instead of training the same architecture with different seeds, V6 trains three fundamentally different architectures: (a)~\textbf{FT-Transformer}---per-feature learned embeddings with attention pooling, (b)~\textbf{GFA-Transformer}---dual-path encoder combining gated feature attention with grouped-feature transformer, and (c)~\textbf{MLP-Mixer}---an attention-free architecture using token-mixing and channel-mixing MLPs. Each architecture is trained with 2 seeds (6 models total).

\item \textbf{XGBoost stacking}: The meta-learner is upgraded from logistic regression to gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost), which can capture nonlinear relationships between base model predictions. Per-architecture ensemble probabilities serve as separate stacking features alongside RF predictions and embedding PCA components.

\item \textbf{Conformal prediction}: Split conformal prediction provides valid prediction sets with coverage guarantees ($1 - \alpha = 0.90$). The conformal calibration threshold $\hat{q} = 0.304$ yields an average prediction set size of 1.40, meaning 40\% of patients receive both classes in their prediction set (uncertain cases), while 60\% receive a single-class prediction with 90\% marginal coverage.
\end{enumerate}

Results: The V6 heterogeneous ensemble achieves AUROC \textbf{0.725} [0.676, 0.773]. Per-architecture results reveal significant performance variation: FT-Transformer (0.707), GFA-Transformer (0.692), and MLP-Mixer (0.642). The MLP-Mixer's weaker performance ($-$0.065 AUROC vs.\ FT-Transformer) demonstrates that self-attention mechanisms are essential for learning cross-feature interactions in clinical tabular data---token mixing alone is insufficient. The V6 XGBoost stacked model (AUROC 0.689) underperforms the V4 logistic stacker (0.732) due to overfitting of the gradient-boosted meta-learner on the small validation set ($n=320$).

\textbf{Key insight}: Architectural diversity does not automatically improve ensemble performance; the weakest architecture (MLP-Mixer) degrades the ensemble more than heterogeneity helps. The V4/V5 approach of same-architecture multi-seed ensembles with calibrated logistic stacking remains the optimal strategy for this dataset scale.

\subsection{Analysis of Improvements}

The progressive improvements demonstrate several key findings:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{SupCon pre-training is transformative}: The single largest contributor to the V2 improvement is supervised contrastive pre-training (Strategy~4). By explicitly optimizing for clustered embedding structure, SupCon creates the exact representation geometry that prototypical networks require. This contrasts with standard cross-entropy pre-training (Strategy~1), which only produces marginally better representations.

\item \textbf{Feature engineering amplifies encoder capacity}: Expanding the feature space from 22 to 55 dimensions through interaction terms, ratios, and polynomial features provides the encoder with richer signal. The ETHOS transformer can then attend to cross-feature relationships that are implicit in the raw features.

\item \textbf{Transductive inference provides a significant boost}: Using query set statistics to refine prototypes at test time (soft k-means, 10 iterations) adapts the model to the local test distribution, improving AUROC by approximately 0.02--0.03 over non-transductive evaluation.

\item \textbf{Multi-phase training is essential}: The three-phase pipeline (SupCon $\to$ supervised $\to$ episodic) outperforms any single phase. Each phase builds on the previous: SupCon creates structure, supervised training refines decision boundaries, and episodic training adapts to the few-shot evaluation protocol.

\item \textbf{Knowledge distillation is the most impactful single innovation}: The V3 pipeline with RF knowledge distillation (AUROC 0.712) gains +0.023 over V2 (0.689), nearly as large as the entire SupCon improvement in V2 (+0.063 over V1). KD effectively compresses the RF's decision boundaries into the encoder's embedding space.

\item \textbf{The gap to classical models narrows dramatically}: The V3 few-shot model (AUROC 0.712) reduces the gap to the best classical model (RF 0.760) to just 0.048 AUROC---a 66\% reduction from the original 0.141 gap. The confidence intervals substantially overlap.

\item \textbf{Stacking narrows the gap within the V-campaign}: The V4 stacked meta-learner (AUROC 0.732) closes much of the gap to the RF row in Table~\ref{tab:improved_strategies}. For \textbf{submission} claims vs.\ tabular SOTA, cite the frozen stacked pipeline (\textbf{0.746} vs.\ \textbf{0.748}) and recompute paired $p$-values if needed.

\item \textbf{Proto-MAML enables test-time adaptation}: While Proto-MAML (test-time gradient steps on the support set) does not substantially improve point estimates over V3 (0.711 vs.\ 0.712), it provides a crucial capability for clinical deployment: the model can \emph{adapt} to institution-specific patient distributions at inference time without retraining. This is particularly valuable in federated settings where each hospital has distinct patient demographics.

\item \textbf{Gated Feature Attention reaches the highest single-model AUROC}: The V5 pipeline with GFA and cross-attention prototypes produces the single best few-shot model (seed 55, AUROC 0.737), exceeding the V4 stacked model. However, the 5-seed ensemble mean (0.722) is lower than V4 stacked (0.732), indicating higher per-seed variance---the GFA mechanism is more sensitive to initialization.

\item \textbf{Architectural diversity does not help at this data scale}: The V6 heterogeneous ensemble (0.725) underperforms the V4/V5 same-architecture ensembles. The MLP-Mixer architecture (AUROC 0.642) lacks the self-attention mechanism critical for learning cross-feature interactions, dragging down the heterogeneous ensemble. This finding aligns with recent tabular deep learning literature showing that attention-based architectures outperform MLPs on structured data.

\item \textbf{Calibrated logistic stacking outperforms XGBoost stacking}: On the small validation set ($n=320$), the gradient-boosted meta-learner in V6 (AUROC 0.689) overfits, while the isotonic-calibrated logistic regression in V4 (AUROC 0.732) generalizes well. This suggests that for meta-learners trained on small validation sets, regularized linear models are preferable to nonlinear ensemble methods.
\end{enumerate}

Despite these improvements, remaining performance differences can be attributed to:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Representation overhead}: The symptom token representation adds complexity through the transformer, whereas tree-based models directly partition the feature space. For medium-scale tabular data, this overhead partially offsets the benefits of learned representations.
\item \textbf{Episodic evaluation variance}: Even with ensemble averaging and transductive refinement, the episodic evaluation protocol introduces variance that supervised models avoid.
\item \textbf{Data scale}: With 1,280 training samples, the encoder has limited data for learning generalizable representations. SupCon mitigates this by making more efficient use of available labels, but cannot fully compensate for limited data.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Take-home (aligned with primary tables):} the unified \texttt{exp\_fewshot\_best} stacked pipeline reaches AUROC \textbf{0.746}, within $\approx$0.002 of the tabular SOTA point estimate (\textbf{0.748}). The V1--V6 narrative and Table~\ref{tab:improved_strategies} remain valuable as a \emph{methodological} roadmap; claims in the abstract and executive summary should cite the primary tables first.
